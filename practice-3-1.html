Practice · Lesson 3.1

Same Wave, Two Views

The Mind in the Light of Modern and Spiritual Sciences · Advanced

A chord on a piano can be heard as it unfolds in time, or described as a short list of frequencies. Both descriptions are complete, and they describe one wave. Build a chord and watch both views move together, rebuild a wave from its spectrum alone, then practise telling the five kinds of vṛtti apart.

Part I

One chord, two views

Practice: not scored

A wave can be described as it unfolds in time, ψ(t), or as the frequencies it contains, Ψ(f) (§3). Switch notes on and off, change how strong each one is, and watch both views. They are always the same wave.

Part II

Nothing is lost

Practice: not scored

The lesson says the two views are completely equivalent: apply the Fourier transform, apply it backwards, and the same wave returns (§7). Try it. Choose a wave, transform it, then rebuild it from the spectrum alone. After that, see what happens when only part of the spectrum is kept.

What this shows, and what it does not

It shows that for any wave, the time description and the spectrum are two complete descriptions of one signal. Nothing is added and nothing is lost in going either way, provided the whole spectrum is kept, phases included.

It does not show that the mind is made of fixed sine waves, or that Consciousness is a spectrum. The lesson reads ordinary experience as the temporal view, and describes the timeless ground, Brahman, Ātman or Śiva, as the spectral domain (§3). That reading is the lesson's teaching. This page gives only the mathematics it is built on. A vṛtti also arises, lasts and subsides, so its spectrum is spread over a range, as the last wave shows.

Part III

Name that vṛtti

Scored: first pick counts

The Yoga Sūtras sort every vṛtti into five kinds (§5). They are told apart by their relation to an object, not by how vivid they feel. Read each moment and say which kind of vṛtti it is. Your first pick on each moment is the one that counts.

Everyday moments, after the lesson’s own examples.

The five, by relation to the object

The point of learning the five is not to file your experiences but to recognise them: to notice a vṛtti as something the mind produces, and so to be no longer identified with it (§5, Recognizing vṛttis). Real life is less tidy than these moments: the boundaries blur, and a worry can turn out to be right. The moments here were chosen to be clear under the lesson's own criteria.
