The Mind in the Light of Modern and Spiritual Sciences · Lesson 5.6

Draw up the rope

Swami Vivekananda tells of a minister shut at the top of a tower, freed by a beetle, a drop of honey and four threads of growing strength. The lesson reads the story as the working image of why the practices are taken up in sequence, from the most accessible to the most subtle. Here you try to help him, read the four links against the four practice-families, and see where the steadiness of the hands comes in.

I. The tower and the ropeII. The four linksIII. The steadiness of the hands

I. The tower and the rope

The minister has no stair, and no one can hand him the rope. His wife stands below with a long rope, stout twine, pack thread, silken thread, a beetle and a little honey. You are her hands. Try what anyone would try, then do what the story does, and notice what you can set and what you cannot.

Breath→Nerve currents→Thoughts→Prāṇa→freedom

1. At the foot of the tower

Send each thing up to him by hand, then let him try the walls himself.

2. Set the conditions

The husband told her what to do with the beetle. Do each of these. The first two can go in either order.

3. Follow the thread

Once the minister holds the silken thread, tie the next link to its foot and he will draw it up. Which comes next?

Who did what

What was set
  • The silken thread tied to the beetle.
  • A drop of honey, and the beetle pointed upward.
  • Each next link tied on and followed up, always the frailest still available.
What lifted
  • The beetle’s climb. You had no button for it: it was drawn by the scent, not driven.

“The ego cannot climb the tower; the ego is the tower. What climbs is something drawn from above, using the threads the practitioner has patiently put in place.”

The rope is at the window. In the story the minister now climbs down it and is free. This page does not show that, and cannot: the lesson places the last link at the verge of the {9} → {0} threshold, and what lies beyond it is not something a screen delivers. The order was fixed by accessibility: the frailest first, the strongest last, and the strongest could never have been sent up first.

II. The four links and the four families

Three sources say one thing: the story, the sūtra and the practice-families you already hold. Open each link in turn and read across. Then test your ear on the lesson’s own examples.

Which link is it?

Each card is a moment from Lesson 5.6 or its neighbours. Choose the link it belongs to, or say it is not a link at all.

Read across the chain

Two things to notice. Attention appears twice, at the pack thread and at the rope: the second is its deepest register. And action appears nowhere in the four rows. It is not a fifth link; it is the subject of the next part.

III. The steadiness of the hands

One practice-family has no link of its own. The Technology of Action and Offering (5.4) does the work upstream, in the fifteen or sixteen waking hours outside formal practice. The lesson’s phrase is that karma yoga is the steadiness of the hands that hold the chain. Compare two ways the same hours can be lived. Nothing here is counted or scored.

Setting conditions, or being lifted?

The lesson’s principle is that the practitioner sets the conditions and consents, and the lift is anugraha. Sort a few moments from the story and the emblem’s motto.

The motto beneath the emblem

तन्नो हंसः प्रचोदयात् tan no haṃsaḥ pracodayāt

“May that Swan, the Supreme Self, awaken our higher understanding.” The verse does not say “may we climb”; it says “may the Swan awaken us.” The four Yogas are our consent. The lifting is from above, and the Swan is not produced by the four.

Lesson 5.3 adds the recognitional reading: the operative power is “the same self-luminous awareness that is operating right now as you read these words.” Both readings stand in the lesson.
