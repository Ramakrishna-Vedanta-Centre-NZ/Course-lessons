The Mind in the Light of Modern and Spiritual Sciences · Lesson 1.1

Given or brought?

Every perception is a meeting of two things: what is given, ψx, and what you bring, ψm. Here you will sort ordinary moments into the two, hold the same given while the person changes, and then watch the two waves of the lesson become one ripple.

I. Sort the moment

Read each statement and decide whether it belongs to what is given or to what is brought. Tap a button on the statement. You can try again as often as you like, and nothing is scored.

Given · ψx

What you could write down as bare description: who said what, what happened, what was visible or audible. Descriptive, not interpretive (§13).

Brought · ψm

Your mood, the memories it touches, what you expect, fear or hope, the labels and history you carry (§10, §13).

      Two things the lesson says, held together

      Experience comes first, before it is named or judged (§9). And what feels like direct observation is always, at least partly, interpretation (§6, §14.2). So this sorting does not claim that a pure, unmixed given can ever be had. It is a practice of description: afterwards, you separate what you could simply state from what you added. In the living moment the two are already one ripple (§10).

      Nor is ψm a fault to be removed. Memory, mood, expectation and meaning are part of how the mind knows (§10), and thought is “valuable, but partial” (§6).

      II. Same given, different brought

      “The scene in front of each person is essentially the same. What each one experiences is shaped by what they bring” (§10). Choose a scene, then switch the person. Watch which side changes and which side does not.

          What this shows, and what it does not

          The same given can be met by different people and become different situations. That is the lesson's explanation of why “two people in the same room can have entirely different experiences” (§10). It does not say that one of them is wrong. The invitation of §14.4 is gentler: once you can see that a difficult experience is the meeting of an outer ψx and an inner ψm, it becomes workable, because you have a part in it.

          III. Two waves, one ripple

          The lesson's picture (§10, Figure 1.1.b): one wave comes in from outside, another arises from your own inner movement. Where they meet they combine into a single ripple, and that ripple is what you experience as “the situation”. It is not the original wave.

          Whose ψm?

          A picture, not a measurement

          The shapes above are drawn for illustration. There is no scale, no unit and nothing measured: the lesson says only that the two combine into one ripple. How the picture is made precise comes later, in Module 3 (§3).

          One question stays open, and this page does not try to close it. If every perception is ψx mixed with ψm, what is the knowing in which the mixing appears? The lesson says this “cannot be answered by more thinking” and that it has to be looked into (§11). That looking is the work of Lesson 1.2.

          Your own turn comes on paper: the two-column experiment of §13, done with a real reaction of your own. A page cannot do that looking for you.
