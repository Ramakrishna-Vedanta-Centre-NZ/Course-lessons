The Mind in the Light of Modern and Spiritual Sciences · Lesson 5.3

The Antarāya Clinic

Patañjali names nine ways the inner instrument can fail, four signs that arise with them, and one rise in the signal-to-noise ratio that dissolves them. Here you will meet nine invented practitioners, name what is going wrong for each, see what comes with it and where the lesson says to begin, and then watch why relief and realisation are not two goals.

I. Name the obstacle

YS 1.30 lists nine antarāyas, each a distinct failure-mode of the inner instrument with its own signature. Read what each practitioner says. Name the antarāya, then its dominant guṇa as the lesson’s table gives it. The practitioners are invented.

“Mixed” indicates an antarāya that can present with either tamasic or rajasic signature depending on the system; it is not a third guṇa-category (§3.5).

Not a failure of will

Each antarāya is a structural failure-mode: it names which component of the instrument is malfunctioning and how (§3.5). Read beside the Saṁyama Table, the Antarāya Table tells the practitioner what is going wrong; the Saṁyama Table tells them what comes right when the antarāya dissolves.

A learning aid, not an assessment. The practitioners are invented, and nothing here diagnoses anyone. Illness belongs first with a doctor; distress with a qualified professional or someone you trust.

Where does this practitioner begin?

“The practitioner whose Saṁyama is failing cannot simply try harder. The failure has structural causes, and the structure tells us where to begin” (§7). Choose a case, read it again, and choose what comes first.

Rhythm prepares; attention crosses

“In both presentations, the principle is the same: Saṁyama begins where the rhythm-and-sound family of Lesson 5.2 has prepared the ground. Once the threshold is crossed, japa with artha-bhāvanam, Patañjali’s prescribed practice, is the natural entry into dhāraṇā” (§7).

A learning aid, not an assessment. It shows the lesson’s reasoning on invented cases and gives no personal advice. Illness comes first to medical care, not to practice.

III. One rise, two readings

YS 1.29: “Thence comes the realisation of the inward-turned awareness, and the dissolution of the obstacles.” Before you watch it, decide what you think the sūtra is saying.

An invented practitioner

“First I want the restlessness and the dullness to go. Realisation can be a separate project, later.”

What does YS 1.29 say about these two?

    Masked by noise.

    One control on this panel.

    There is a single term being changed: n(t), the noise of the inner instrument. Let the four effects accumulate one at a time and watch both sides.

    Patañjali’s ca

    “The practitioner is not asked to choose between symptom-relief and self-realisation. The practice that reduces anxiety, dullness, doubt, and instability is the practice that makes the substrate perceptible. The same procedure raises SNR; raised SNR has both faces. There is no trade-off, no detour. That is what Patañjali’s ca is doing” (§3.4).

    A learning aid, not an assessment. A qualitative picture of the lesson’s reading of YS 1.29; it measures nothing.
